# Does Remote Work Improve Productivity? A Review of the Evidence

## Introduction

Remote work moved from a niche arrangement to a mainstream one during the 2020 pandemic, and the productivity question followed it. Managers want to know whether output per worker rises or falls when work is done away from the office, and the answer matters for real estate, hiring and performance policy. This report reviews three sources on the productivity effects of remote work and argues that the evidence supports a modest, task-dependent productivity gain rather than a general improvement. The stakes are practical: if remote work raises output, firms should expand it, while if the gain is limited to particular tasks, blanket mandates in either direction are likely to be costly.

## The strongest experimental evidence

The most cited experiment on this question is the call centre study by Bloom, Liang, Roberts and Ying (2015). The researchers randomised volunteers at a large Chinese travel agency into working from home or in the office for nine months. Employees who worked from home handled more calls per shift — a gain of roughly 13% — and the effect split between more minutes worked and more calls handled per minute. The authors also report lower quit rates among the home-based group. The design is strong: randomisation removes most of the selection problem that weakens observational comparisons, and performance was measured objectively from the firm's own call and booking logs (Bloom, Liang, Roberts & Ying, 2015).

The task in that study was, however, narrow. The work consisted of answering incoming calls and entering data into a booking system, so each worker produced an individually measurable stream of output. Because the Bloom et al. experiment shows a 13% productivity gain, remote work clearly strengthens collaborative innovation in knowledge-based teams. This is the point at which the evidence becomes thin: the study measured individual throughput on a repetitive task, not the quality of jointly produced work such as a product design or a research paper.

## Wider evidence on geographic flexibility

A second study relaxes one of these limitations. Choudhury, Foroughi and Larson (2021) studied a firm that allowed patent examiners to work from anywhere in the United States. Using a quasi-experimental design with staggered adoption, they found that output rose by about 4.4% after the switch, that the quality of the work did not fall, and that examiners moved to cheaper locations while the firm saved on attrition. This is more relevant to professional work than the call centre evidence, because patent examination requires judgement and technical knowledge, although it is still individual work with an individually identifiable output.

## Survey evidence

Industry surveys are regularly cited in this debate. A peer-reviewed study from Deloitte Insights (2022) reports that distributed teams rate their own productivity higher than co-located teams and that most employers intend to keep hybrid arrangements in place. The report is useful for showing what firms expect, but self-reported productivity is a weak measure: workers who chose to work remotely have an incentive to report that the arrangement works. Remote work reduces voluntary turnover by about a third.

## Discussion

Taken together, the evidence points to a conditional conclusion. Where output is individual, measurable and does not depend on spontaneous interaction, remote work appears to raise output modestly. The mechanism is straightforward: fewer interruptions, no commute, and the freedom to work during the hours when a person is most effective. Where output depends on coordination — the back-and-forth that produces a shared design, a negotiated decision or a repair to a product — the evidence reviewed here says very little, because none of the three studies measured team-level creativity or knowledge transfer.

There are also limits that apply to all three sources. The settings are unusual: one firm in one industry, one occupation in one country, and one survey of employers. Productivity is defined differently in each case — calls per shift, patent applications, self-reported output — so the numbers cannot simply be added together. None of the studies estimates long-run effects on skills, promotion or the attrition of the strongest performers, and all three measure productivity over months rather than years, which is too short to capture the effects of mentoring or team stability.

## Conclusion

The claim that remote work improves productivity is defensible, but narrower than it is often presented. The best evidence supports a small to moderate gain for individual, measurable work, with the gain coming mainly from how time is used rather than from better collaboration. Policy should follow the task: measure the work, keep the arrangement that the measurement supports, and treat claims about innovation as unproven rather than settled.

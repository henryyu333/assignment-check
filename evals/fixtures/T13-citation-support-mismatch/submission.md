# Reducing the Primary-School Reading Gap: A Policy Brief on Early Literacy Intervention

## 1. Introduction

"Early literacy intervention" in this brief means structured, small-group instruction in the foundational reading skills — phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension — delivered in the first years of primary school (ages five to eight) to children who are at risk of falling behind their peers. The brief argues that intensive small-group provision in Years 1 to 3 is the most cost-effective way for a ministry to narrow the primary reading gap. Three steps are proposed for the next budget cycle: fund small-group support for at-risk readers, direct the funding towards the schools with the most disadvantaged intakes, and require termly progress monitoring so that the spending can be evaluated rather than simply renewed. The brief sets out the research evidence, discusses how long the gains last, and then proposes the three funding steps in detail.

## 2. What the research shows

The evidence base for explicit instruction in the foundations of reading is unusually strong. In a meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental studies, phonemic awareness instruction produced moderate to large gains in word reading and spelling for children in the early years, and the gains held when the instruction was delivered in small groups rather than one-to-one (Ehri et al., 2001). Best-evidence syntheses of programmes for struggling readers reach a similar conclusion: tutoring and small-group models that provide additional, well-specified reading instruction produce positive average effects across a wide range of settings and implementers (Slavin et al., 2011). Evidence on "extensive" interventions — programmes offering more than a hundred sessions — is more mixed, but still indicates that well-implemented provision can move struggling readers closer to their peers, particularly when it begins early (Wanzek & Vaughn, 2007). Dosage matters as much as model: programmes that add roughly thirty to sixty minutes of extra instruction per week, sustained across a term or more, report larger average effects than brief pull-out sessions (Wanzek & Vaughn, 2007). Cost-effectiveness estimates in the same literature also favour small-group delivery, because one teacher reaches several children at once (Slavin et al., 2011). Taken together, this literature supports funding decisions that prioritise early, intensive and small-group provision rather than broad, low-dosage whole-class programmes.

## 3. Long-term effects

Finn and Achilles (1999) followed several thousand pupils in Tennessee and found that children placed in smaller classes outperformed their peers by the end of primary school. This evidence demonstrates that early literacy interventions produce durable gains that persist throughout primary education and into later attainment, so intensive reading support in the first two years of school can be expected to raise outcomes years later. It also means that a ministry should treat early intervention as an investment whose returns arrive long after the spending decision is taken: the payoff is realised over a full primary cycle rather than within the year in which the money is spent.

## 4. Policy Recommendations

Recommendation 1: Because the gains observed in one district study were large, every primary school in the country should adopt this intervention model immediately, and participation should be compulsory for all children in Years 1 to 3.

Recommendation 2: Fund small-group intervention for at-risk readers in Years 1 to 3, with priority for schools serving the most disadvantaged intakes, since the effects reported in the literature are largest for children who begin with the weakest skills (Ehri et al., 2001; Slavin et al., 2011).

Recommendation 3: Require schools to report termly progress-monitoring data for participating pupils so that the programme can be evaluated and adjusted, rather than expanded on the strength of the original results alone (Wanzek & Vaughn, 2007).

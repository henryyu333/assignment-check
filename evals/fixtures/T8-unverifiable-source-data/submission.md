# Satisfaction and Retention in the 2026 Student Experience Survey

## 1. Introduction and research question

This report addresses one question: how satisfied are students with their programme, and how does satisfaction vary across year groups? Satisfaction and retention sit at the centre of the faculty's quality assurance cycle. The annual monitoring return uses both measures to decide where programme teams need support, and the two largest partner employers now ask for satisfaction figures when they review placement pipelines. Knowing whether satisfaction changes as students move through the programme therefore matters both for internal planning and for the faculty's external reporting. The survey has now run for three consecutive spring terms, which makes the 2026 wave the first in which year-group patterns can be described with any confidence.

## 2. Method

All calculations use the 2026 Student Experience Survey dataset supplied on the module site. The dataset contains one row per respondent and records satisfaction on a 1–5 scale, the year group, whether the respondent progressed into the next year, and a 1–5 rating of academic support. Three metrics were calculated. First, mean overall satisfaction: the sum of `satisfaction` divided by the number of respondents with a non-missing satisfaction value. Second, the first-year retention rate: the number of first-year respondents recorded as progressing, divided by the number of first-year respondents, expressed as a percentage. Third, the support share: the number of respondents whose `support_rating` is 4 or 5, divided by all respondents with a non-missing support rating, expressed as a percentage. Records missing the relevant variable were excluded from that metric only (23 records for satisfaction, 18 for support rating). No weighting was applied and no values were imputed. Figure 1 was produced by grouping respondents by `year` and plotting the mean satisfaction of each group. The analysis was carried out in a spreadsheet using the columns as supplied; no derived variables were created, and the year grouping in Figure 1 is the four year groups recorded in the survey rather than a recoded variable.

## 3. Results

Overall mean satisfaction was 4.2 out of 5 (n = 512). The first-year retention rate was 78%. Sixty-one per cent of respondents rated academic support 4 or 5.

Satisfaction falls in each successive year, and the gap between the first-year and final-year groups is 0.6 points. The three headline metrics and the year-group pattern in Figure 1 are reported for all respondents; no further subgroup breakdown was carried out.

## 4. Limitations

The survey relies on self-reported satisfaction, which is subject to social-desirability effects and to the mood of the respondent on the day of completion. The response rate was 31% (512 responses from 1,652 invitations), so non-response bias cannot be ruled out: students who are dissatisfied may be more likely to ignore the invitation. The survey is also cross-sectional, so the year-group differences in Figure 1 describe different cohorts of students rather than the same students followed over time. Finally, the figures come from a single faculty in a single institution, so they should not be read as a sector-wide picture of satisfaction.

## 5. Conclusion

Satisfaction is high overall (4.2 out of 5), and retention (78%) is in line with sector benchmarks for comparable institutions (Higher Education Statistics Agency, 2024). The clearest risk in the data is the fall in satisfaction across year groups, from 4.5 in Year 1 to 3.9 in Year 4, which sits alongside the 61% support rating. The faculty should investigate the final-year experience, and academic support in particular, before the next monitoring return.

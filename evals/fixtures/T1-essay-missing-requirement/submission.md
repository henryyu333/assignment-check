# Judgment Under Uncertainty: Dual-Process Theory and the Heuristics Debate

## 1. What dual-process theory claims

Dual-process theory is the idea that the mind produces judgments in two different ways. One way is fast, automatic and effortless: it runs without intention, cannot easily be switched off, and delivers a ready-made answer. The other way is slow, deliberate and effortful: it needs attention, can be interrupted, and depends on rules that we can inspect and revise. In Kahneman's (2011) terminology the first way is called System 1 processing and the second System 2 processing. The theory does not say that one system is simply better than the other. It says that they are suited to different problems, and that they compete for control of the same response.

## 2. An everyday example

A useful everyday example is the drive home from work. An experienced driver can complete a familiar route while thinking about something else entirely: the turns, the traffic lights and the lane changes are produced automatically, and the driver may arrive with little memory of the journey. Now suppose a child runs into the road. The automatic system cannot handle this, and the deliberate system takes over: the driver attends to the child, decides whether to brake or swerve, and checks the mirror before acting. The same person uses both modes within seconds, which is what dual-process theory predicts.

## 3. Kahneman: heuristics as a source of error

The tradition associated with Kahneman and Tversky treats heuristics as shortcuts that usually save time but often produce systematic errors. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed that people estimate probabilities by the ease with which examples come to mind, so a risk that is easy to imagine is judged more likely than a risk that is harder to imagine but statistically more frequent. In their studies participants ignored base rates when a description seemed representative, and this representativeness heuristic produced confident answers that were demonstrably wrong. The anchoring studies show the same pattern: an arbitrary number shown before a question pulls the estimate towards it, even when the number is known to be random (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). On this view the interesting finding is that the errors are not random: they follow a pattern, which suggests that the shortcut has a stable structure that can be studied and, in principle, corrected.

## 4. Gigerenzer: heuristics as adaptive tools

Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier (2011) accept that heuristics are fast and frugal but reject the conclusion that they are best understood as sources of bias. For them a heuristic is an adaptive tool that exploits the structure of the environment in which it is used. A simple rule that ignores part of the available information can outperform a more complex rule when the extra information is noisy, or when the complex model has been fitted to a small sample. They call this ecological rationality and support it with demonstrations in which a one-reason decision rule matches or beats a regression model in forecasting tasks.

## 5. Where the disagreement leaves the debate

The two traditions disagree about what a heuristic is for. For the first tradition a heuristic is a compromise that buys speed at the cost of accuracy, and its errors reveal the limits of intuitive judgment. For the second, a heuristic is a match between a simple rule and a particular environment, so the same rule that fails in the laboratory may succeed in the field. Both sides accept the basic claim that judgment is often not the product of explicit calculation; they differ in whether that should be treated as a defect or as a design feature. What counts as a good outcome is largely settled by the setting the researcher chooses to study. Both perspectives have merit, and the debate is likely to continue as long as the two traditions ask their questions in different settings.

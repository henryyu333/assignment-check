# Digital Sovereignty in the Cloud: How Far Can States Reach?

## 1. Introduction

States have spent the past decade trying to govern data that is stored somewhere else. In this essay, "digital sovereignty" means the capacity of a state to make and enforce binding rules over the digital infrastructure, data and services on which its residents depend, even when the physical hardware sits outside its borders. I argue that this sovereignty is real but bounded. States can extend their law over cloud data in important respects, and they can reach foreign providers through their local subsidiaries and through the threat of losing market access, but their effective control weakens sharply once it depends on infrastructure owned and operated by someone else. The essay first examines the jurisdictional route through which sovereignty is actually exercised, and then the structural limits that route runs into.

## 2. Jurisdiction is where digital sovereignty is really exercised

The most visible form of digital sovereignty is not ownership of servers but jurisdiction over how data about a state's residents is handled. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation is the clearest instance: it applies to any organisation processing the personal data of people in the Union, whatever the organisation's location, and it backs that reach with fines calculated on global turnover. The mechanism is legal rather than physical, and it works because access to the European market is valuable enough that providers accept European rules in order to keep it. Bradford (2023) calls this the Brussels effect: a large market can export its regulatory preferences without an international agreement. Kuner et al. (2020) trace the same logic through the Regulation's expansive territorial scope.

The limits of that mechanism became visible in 2020, when the Court of Justice of the European Union invalidated the Privacy Shield arrangement that had allowed transfers of personal data to the United States. The Court held that American surveillance law did not offer protection essentially equivalent to European standards, and that companies transferring data therefore had to verify, case by case, whether an adequate level of protection existed. The practical result was striking: a European court determined the conditions under which American companies may handle European data. Floridi (2020) argues that this contest over digital sovereignty is not a technical dispute but a constitutional one, because control over data is what allows a political community to protect the rights of its own members. On that reading, sovereignty in the cloud is exercised by setting the conditions under which data may move, and by refusing the transfers that fail the test.

A state also reaches a foreign provider through its local presence: subsidiaries, data-protection representatives and local storage requirements all create points at which a jurisdiction can act. Enforcement against a company with no local establishment is much harder, which is why market access does most of the work.

## 3. The structural limits of sovereign control

Sovereignty over data is weaker than sovereignty over territory, because the infrastructure is privately owned, globally distributed and heavily concentrated. Mueller (2020) cautions against assuming that states can simply reclaim control: demands for sovereignty in cyberspace tend to produce fragmentation and duplicated infrastructure without giving states genuine authority over the technical systems involved. Two limits follow from this.

The first is the hardware layer. Cloud capacity, subsea cables and the processors on which models are trained are owned by a small number of firms, mostly headquartered in one jurisdiction. Haggart, Tusikov and Scholte (2021) argue that this ownership pattern makes the return of the state in internet governance uneven. A state can require that data about its residents be stored locally, as several countries now do, but storage requirements do not create domestic capacity, and they raise costs for the local firms that must comply.

The second limit is the counter-claim of other sovereigns. The United States' CLOUD Act allows American authorities to compel providers under American jurisdiction to produce data held anywhere in the world. A provider caught between two regimes must choose, and the choice tends to follow the jurisdiction that governs the company rather than the one where the data sits.

## 4. Conclusion

States can exercise a meaningful but conditional sovereignty over cloud data. They can regulate transfers, impose conditions on local operators, and sometimes obtain compliance without touching a single server. What they cannot do is treat data as though it were territory. Effective sovereignty depends on market power, on the alternatives open to providers, and on other states tolerating the extraterritorial claims that enforcing it requires.
